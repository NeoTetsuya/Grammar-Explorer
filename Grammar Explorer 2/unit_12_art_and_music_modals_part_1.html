Modals: Ability, Possibility, & Requests

Express artistic talent, explore logical deductions, ask for permission politely, and format creative titles accurately.

1. Ability: Past, Present, and Future

Universal Syntactic Rules for Modals

  • No Endings: Never add -s, -es, -ed, or -ing to a modal.
  • Base Verb: Always use the base form directly after modals.
  • Questions: Invert subject & modal. Never use do/does/did!
  • Negative Can: 3 valid forms: can not, cannot, and can't.

Can & Could

  • Present / Future Ability (Can) Expresses present talent or future possibility.
    "The artist can create sculptures from trash."
  • General Past Ability (Could) Refers to general past ability.
    "Jon could paint well when he was young."

Be Able To & Future Constraint

Flexible Conjugation: Subject + be (not) able to + base verb.
• Present: They are able to read the text. • Past: We were able to watch the show. • Future: He will be able to stay.
Critical Constraint: No "Will Can"! Never say will can. Always use will be able to for new or learned future abilities!

2. Possibility & Logical Conclusions

Possibility: May, Might, Could

Weak Uncertainty (May / Might / Could): Expresses an uncertain possibility in the present/future.
"The song may become a big hit. I don't know."
Strong Negative Certainty (Couldn't): When you are almost certain something is NOT true or possible.
"He couldn't be in Paris; I saw him in London."
May be vs. Maybe May be = Modal + Verb (e.g. They may be home.)
Maybe = Adverb at start (e.g. Maybe they are home.)

Logical Conclusions: Must & Must Not

Positive Deduction (Must): Strong certainty based on clear present evidence.
"I hear you laughing. You must be happy!"
Negative Deduction (Must Not): Strong negative conclusion based on evidence.
"Barbara must not eat meat; she never buys it."
Fact vs. Deduction Rule Do NOT use must for known, stated facts! Use simple tenses instead (e.g., The gallery is closed today; the sign says so.)

3. Permission & Requests

Permission: May, Could, Can

Formality Scale (Asking): May I (most formal) > Could I (polite) > Can I (informal).
"Could I use your camera tomorrow?"
Responding Constraint! When granting or refusing permission in response to a question, use can or may. Never respond with could!
"Could my children stay?" → "Sure they can." "Sure they could."

Requests: Would, Could, Can, Will

Making Requests: Would you & Could you are more polite than Will you & Can you.
"Would you please turn down the music?"
Responding & Cushioning • Affirmative: Use can or will (never could/would).
• Declining politely: Avoid flat "No". Use cushioning phrases like "I'm sorry, I can't..." or "I'm afraid...".

Writing Focus: Formatting Titles of Works

When writing an art or media review, follow strict punctuation rules for titles based on length:

Italics (Longer Works) Use italics for movies, books, paintings, television series, and albums.
• Gravity, • Mona Lisa, • Dracula
Quotation Marks (Shorter Works) Use quotation marks ("...") for poems, songs, short stories, and single TV episodes.
• "The Journey", • "Yesterday"
