Lesson 1 • Charts 5.1 - 5.3

Present Perfect: Statements, Questions & Time Adverbs

Have/Has + Past Participle • 4 Core Functions • Adverb Positions

4 Core Uses of the Present Perfect

1. Recently Completed Action:

Focuses on an action that ended right before now:
"The plane has arrived." | "Ellen has returned from India."

2. Incomplete Time Period (Today, This Week, This Month):

The action occurs within an unfinished period of time:
"John hasn’t called today." (It is currently only 4:00 p.m.)

3. Repeated Past Events at an Indefinite Time:

Actions that happened one or more times, when the exact time is unstated:
"I have read that book three times." | "Our professor has gone to Asia many times."

4. Present Result / Past Experience:

The exact moment is unimportant, but the current result or knowledge matters:
"I have washed the dishes." (They are clean now.)
"Nancy has lived in Mexico City." (She has practical knowledge of it now.)

Real English: Compound Verbs with 'And'

When writing two present perfect verbs joined by and, do NOT repeat has or have before the second verb:
"Aasim has recently graduated from college and started a new job."

6 Key Adverbs with Present Perfect

Adverb Meaning & Placement Textbook Example
already Sometime before now (before PP or at end) "We've already seen that movie."
ever / never At any time / at no time before now (before PP) "Have you ever driven a truck?" / "I've never been to London."
just Right before now (before PP) "The plane has just landed."
recently / lately In the recent past (before PP or at end) "Have you seen any good movies lately?"
still Up to now (placed before hasn't / haven't) "She still hasn’t called me."
yet Until now (at the end of negatives and questions) "He hasn’t finished yet." / "Have you called yet?"
Who & What as Subject Questions

• When Who or What acts as the subject, the verb takes third-person singular auxiliary has:
"Who has been to Italy?" | "What has happened this week?"

• Trap: Never use do, does, or did in present perfect questions!

Lesson 2 • Charts 5.4 - 5.5

Present Perfect with For, Since & Past Time Clauses

Duration vs. Starting Point • How Long Questions • Since Clauses

For vs. Since Distinction

Use the present perfect with for or since to describe an action or state that started in the past and continues into the present moment:

Duration / Span FOR + Length of Time

Used with a count of hours, days, months, years, or periods:

• for an hour / for three days

• for a year / for 20 years

• for a long time / for several weeks

"He’s worked at that company for 20 years."

Starting Point SINCE + Specific Point in Time

Used with a specific calendar moment, date, year, or clock time:

• since Tuesday / since 9:00 a.m.

• since April / since 2008

• since yesterday / since this morning

"I’ve known Jack since 2008."

How Long Questions:

Questions asking about continuing duration begin with How long + have/has + Subject + Past Participle?

"A: How long have you lived here? → B: For a long time. / Since 2004."

Past Time Clauses with "Since"

Since can also introduce a dependent past time clause that establishes the starting point:

[Present Perfect Main Clause] + since + [Simple Past Time Clause]

• "I’ve known Rita since I was in high school."

• "She has lived in Mexico since she was 21."

• "Life has changed a lot since personal computers went on sale."

Comma Rule for Since Clauses

• Time Clause First: Follow with a comma:
"Since she was 21, she has lived in Mexico."

• Main Clause First: No comma:
"She has lived in Mexico since she was 21."

Lesson 3 • Charts 5.6 - 5.7

Present Perfect vs. Simple Past

Indefinite vs. Definite Past • Conversational Transition • Incomplete vs. Complete Time

Indefinite vs. Definite Past Time

Grammar Dimension Present Perfect (Indefinite) Simple Past (Definite)
Time Specified? NO (Indefinite / Unstated) YES (Definite / Stated / Understood)
Primary Focus Experience, status, or present result Completed historical moment or event
Examples "They have left." (Not here now)
"Have you seen Dracula?"		 "They left last night at 9:00."
"I saw it last week."
Natural Conversation Opening Pattern:

English speakers commonly initiate conversations with an indefinite general inquiry in the present perfect, and then transition to simple past for details of time, place, and impressions:

A: "Have you ever seen that movie?" (Present Perfect)
B: "Yes, I saw it yesterday with my sister." (Simple Past)
A: "Did you like the special effects?" (Simple Past)

Incomplete vs. Complete Time Spans

1. Ongoing Career / Living Situation vs. Finished Past

• "Meg has worked at the school for 10 years." → She still works there today.

• "Meg worked at the school for 20 years, but she retired last year." → She does not work there anymore.

2. The Clock Rule (This Morning / This Afternoon)

• Present Perfect (Incomplete Period): Said at 11:00 a.m. (still morning) → "I haven't seen George this morning."

• Simple Past (Complete Period): Said at 3:00 p.m. (morning has ended) → "I didn't see George this morning."

Lesson 4 • Charts 5.8 - 5.10

Present Perfect Progressive Aspect

Have/Has Been + V-ing • 3 Uses • Non-Action Ban • Finished vs. Unfinished

3 Major Uses of Present Perfect Progressive

1. Continuing Unfinished Action / Situation:

Started in past and currently underway:
"Inuit people have been living in the Arctic for over a thousand years."

2. Temporary Habit / Exception:

Emphasizes that a recent repeated activity is temporary rather than permanent:
"She's been walking to work this week." (Her car is temporarily broken.)

3. Recently Completed Action with Visible Physical Evidence:

Action ended immediately before now with clear visible results:
"A: Why is there paint on your shirt? → B: I’ve been painting my living room."

Stative / Non-Action Verb Restriction

Do NOT use stative non-action verbs (know, believe, love, own, belong, understand) in the progressive!
✓ They have known Ed for a long time.
✗ They have been knowing Ed for a long time.

Present Perfect vs. Progressive Contrast

Grammar Feature Present Perfect Progressive Present Perfect (Simple)
Completion Status Incomplete / in progress:
"I've been reading a book on Greenland." (Not finished)		 Complete / result:
"I've read a book on Greenland." (Finished)
How Long Questions Use with action verbs:
"How long have you been driving?"		 Use with non-action verbs:
"How long have you owned your car?"
Number of Times / Quantity Avoid with specific counts Required for counting events:
"I've watched three movies this week."
Writing Focus • Connect to Writing

Using Commas Between Items in a Series

Punctuation • The Oxford Comma • Writing About Change

In formal and academic writing, when listing three or more related words, phrases, or clauses in a series, place a comma after every item in the series except the last one.

Series of Nouns

Separate three or more noun items:

✓ "Many birds, butterflies, and small animals have disappeared for the night."
✓ "I've put on a warm sweater, socks, and slippers."

Series of Verb Phrases

Separate multiple consecutive actions:

✓ "The researcher recorded the temperatures, analyzed the data, and published her findings."

Clauses in a Series

Separate multiple independent predications:

✓ "The sea ice has melted, the seals have migrated, and the hunters have faced hardships."
Writing About Change Self-Assessment Checklist:
Used the present perfect to describe recently completed actions and events.
Used for (duration) and since (starting point) correctly.
Used the present perfect progressive for ongoing continuous actions.
Inserted commas between words, phrases, or clauses in a series.
