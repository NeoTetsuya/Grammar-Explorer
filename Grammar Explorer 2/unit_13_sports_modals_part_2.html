1. Necessity, Prohibition, & Lack of Necessity

Necessity: Must, Have To, Have Got To

  • Must (Formal / Official Rules): Used by authority for official laws and regulations.
    "Drivers must wear seat belts."
  • Have To (Everyday Necessity): More common for personal needs.
    "My child is sick; I have to leave early."
  • Have Got To (Conversational): Informal, affirmative only.
    "We've got to go right now!"

Past Necessity & Question Constraints

Past Necessity: HAD TO Never use must or have got to for past necessity!
"I had to leave before class ended." "I must leave yesterday."
Questions: Do / Does / Did + Subject + Have To Use have to for questions. Do not use must or have got to in questions.
"Do you have to leave so soon?"

Prohibition (Not Allowed)

Must not / May not / Can't Expresses that an action is strictly forbidden.
• "Players must not speak during the match."
• "You can't drive without a license."
Past Prohibition Use couldn't for past prohibition:
"I couldn't drive before I turned 16."

Lack of Necessity (Not Necessary)

Don't / Doesn't Have To Expresses that an action is optional; it is not required.
• "I don't have to take the exam today."
Past Lack of Necessity Use didn't have to in the past:
"They didn't have to take tennis lessons."
Have Got To Constraint Have got to has NO negative or past form!

2. Advisability & Expectations

Advisability: Should & Ought To

Giving Advice / Good Ideas: Use should or ought to + base verb.
"You should exercise regularly to stay fit."
Real English Usage Tips: • Should is far more common than ought to.
• Negative: Use shouldn't. (Ought not to is very rare).
• Questions: Use Should we...? (Do not use ought to in questions).

Expectations: Be Supposed To

Present / Future Expectations: am/is/are + supposed to + base verb.
"I'm supposed to arrive early."
Past Expectations (Unfulfilled): Use was/were supposed to for something that was expected, but did NOT happen.
• "It was supposed to rain today."
• "He wasn't supposed to arrive until 10."

Writing Focus: Clear Introductions in Opinion Essays

A compelling opening paragraph for an opinion essay (such as recommending a sport) must accomplish two goals:

1. Relate to Readers Introduce a relatable situation or shared experience to hook the audience.
"We all know we are supposed to exercise to stay in shape, but going to the gym can become boring."
2. State Thesis Clearly Directly state your main point or recommendation using modals.
"If you live near mountains, you definitely ought to try snowboarding."
