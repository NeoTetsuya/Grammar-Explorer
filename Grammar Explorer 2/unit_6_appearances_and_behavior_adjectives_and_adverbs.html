Adjectives & Nouns as Adjectives

Position • Linking Verbs • Singular Rule

Functions & Positions of Adjectives

1. Before Nouns (Attributive Position):

Adjectives describe nouns and typically appear directly in front of them:

• "These cute creatures have become a popular symbol of Australia."

• "The koala has sharp claws and thick fur."

2. After Linking Verbs (Predicative Position):

Adjectives can follow be or other linking verbs to describe the subject:

Linking Verbs: appear, be, become, feel, get, look, seem, smell, sound, taste

• "The koala at the zoo was fierce." | "These leaves are harmful."

• "The soup smells delicious." (NOT: smells deliciously)

3. The Singular Rule:

Adjectives never take a plural -s in English, even when modifying plural nouns:

✓ We visited some ancient towns in Italy.

✗ We visited some ancients towns in Italy.

Nouns Used as Adjectives

A noun can function as an adjective to describe another noun that follows it. When a noun acts as an adjective, it always remains singular:

Original Noun Phrase Noun as Adjective Structure Correct Form
a nurse in a school school + nurse a school nurse
a store that sells shoes shoe + store a shoe store (NOT shoes store)
tickets for planes plane + tickets plane tickets (NOT planes tickets)
a building with 300 apartments apartment + building an apartment building
a manager of three restaurants restaurant + manager a restaurant manager
Common Pluralization Trap

• Even when the final noun is plural, the modifying noun stays singular:
✓ car races (✗ cars races), ✓ grocery bags (✗ groceries bags).

Word Order of Adjectives

The Royal Order (OSASCOMP) • Coordinate vs. Cumulative Punctuation

When multiple adjectives describe the same noun, native English speakers follow a standard hierarchy. We rarely use more than three adjectives together in natural speech or academic writing.

1. Opinion 2. Size 3. Age 4. Shape 5. Color 6. Origin 7. Material 8. Noun as Adj Head Noun
— — new — black — leather — shoes
delicious — — — — — — chocolate cake
— small — round — — — — table
— — old — red — brick — building
beautiful — — — — Chinese — — paintings
gorgeous — — — — — — wedding ring
Coordinate Adjectives Same Category • Use Comma or 'And'

When adjectives give the same kind of information (such as two opinions or two colors), separate them with a comma or and:

• "He is a kind, patient person."

• "He is a kind and patient person."

• "The animal's claws are long and sharp." (after linking verb)

Cumulative Adjectives Different Categories • NO Commas

When adjectives give different types of information (e.g. Size + Color, Age + Material), do NOT use commas:

• "I have a big orange cat." (Size + Color)

• "She has short curly blonde hair." (Size + Shape + Color)

• "We visited an ancient stone castle." (Age + Material)

Lesson 2 • Chart 6.4

Adverbs of Manner & Linking Verbs

Action Modification • Strict Placement Rule • Irregular Adverbs

Adverbs of Manner Functions & Positions

1. Describes Action Verbs:

Adverbs of manner describe how an action is performed. Formed by adding -ly to most adjectives:

• "On her chin and lips she proudly wears a Maori tattoo."

• "People sat quietly and patiently to show their courage."

2. CRITICAL TRAP: Never Separate Verb and Direct Object!

Adverbs of manner follow the verb OR follow the verb + object. They cannot sit between the verb and its object:

✓ He sang the song beautifully. / He sang beautifully.

✗ He sang beautifully the song.

✓ Journalists write their reports quickly.

✗ Journalists write quickly their reports.

Linking Verbs vs. Action Verbs

Verb Type Required Modifier Example
Action Verb Adverb of Manner "She drives carefully."
Linking Verb (be, look, seem, smell, taste, feel, sound) Adjective (NOT adverb!) "She looks happy."
(NOT happily)
Irregular Adverb Forms & False Adverbs

• Identical Forms (No -ly): early, fast, hard, late are both adjectives AND adverbs:
"They eat fast." (✗ fastly) | "He works hard." (✗ hardly means barely!)

• Good → Well: The adverb form of good is well:
"My new laptop works well." (✗ works good).

• -ly Words that are ADJECTIVES: friendly, lonely, ugly, lovely, silly are adjectives, NOT adverbs!

Adverbs of Degree

Intensity Spectrum • Formal vs. Informal • Negative Restriction

Adverbs of degree modify adjectives or other adverbs to make their meaning stronger or weaker. They usually sit directly before the word they modify.

The Adverbs of Degree Spectrum

extremely Strongest
really Strong
very Strong
so Strong
quite Moderate
somewhat Weaker
fairly Weaker
pretty Informal
Register & Formality Formal vs. Informal Pairs

• Formal / Academic: extremely, quite, very, somewhat

"The results of the two laboratory tests were quite different."

• Informal / Conversational: really, pretty, kind of

"I'm pretty sad that my friend moved to Ohio."

Grammar Rule Negative Statement Restrictions

Do NOT use pretty, fairly, or somewhat in negative statements!

✓ She wasn’t very tired. / She wasn’t extremely tired.

✗ She wasn't somewhat tired. / ✗ She wasn't pretty tired.

Writing Focus • Connect to Writing

Using Adjectives & Adverbs in Descriptive Writing

Style • Precision • Avoiding Overloading

Good writers use precise adjectives and adverbs to make their descriptions vivid, informative, and engaging. However, proficient academic and business writers choose words carefully and avoid cluttering sentences with excessive modifiers.

Under-Described (Too Plain)

Lacks descriptive details to help the reader visualize:

"With their arms and legs and tail, they move through the forests."

Overloaded (Too Many Modifiers)

Cluttered with unnecessary words that slow down reading:

"With their big long arms and strong legs and powerful curled tail, they move quickly and gracefully through the beautiful thick Peruvian forests."

Balanced & Effective

Selective, impactful modifiers that convey essential details:

"With their long arms and legs and powerful tail, they move quickly through the forests."
Animal & Character Description Writing Checklist:
Used adjectives before nouns or after linking verbs without plural -s.
Followed the royal adjective order (Opinion → Size → Age → Shape → Color → Origin → Material → Noun-Adj).
Placed adverbs of manner after the verb or object (never between verb and direct object).
Used adjectives after linking verbs (looks unhappy, NOT looks unhappily).
