Personal Pronouns, Possessives & Reflexive Structures

Subjects • Objects • Possessives • Reflexives

Subject & Object Pronouns

Person / Number Subject Pronoun Object Pronoun Reflexive Pronoun
1st Person (Singular) I me myself
2nd Person (Singular/Pl.) you you yourself / yourselves
3rd Person (Masc/Fem/Neut) he / she / it him / her / it himself / herself / itself
1st Person (Plural) we us ourselves
3rd Person (Plural) they them themselves
Pronoun Syntactic Roles:

• Subject Position: Takes the place of a subject noun phrase → "Elena enjoys cycling. She uses her bike to commute."

• Object of Verb: Follows a transitive verb → "Javier met them at the Mexico City airport."

• Object of Preposition: Follows a preposition → "This boarding pass is for him." | "They sat next to us."

Possessive Adjectives vs. Possessive Pronouns

Requires Noun Possessive Adjectives

my, your, his, her, its, our, their

Must be followed by a noun:

"Joe bought his plane ticket."

Replaces Noun Possessive Pronouns

mine, yours, his, hers, its, ours, theirs

Used alone without a noun:

"That coat is not yours. Mine is blue."

Reflexive Pronouns & The "By Myself" Idiom

• Subject = Object: Use when the actor and recipient are identical: "Peter hurt himself in a cycling accident."

• Common Reflexive Verbs: cut, enjoy, feel sorry for, hurt, take care of, make (oneself) comfortable.

• By + Reflexive: Expresses alone or without any help: "She traveled to Mexico by herself." | "The cabin lights turn off by itself."

Crucial Homophone Traps

• Its vs. It's: its = possessive adjective ("The airline lost its luggage"); it's = contraction of it is or it has ("It's delayed for an hour").

• Their vs. There vs. They're: their = possessive ("their tickets"); there = place/location ("over there"); they're = they are ("they're on the train").

Prepositions of Time, Place & Direction

At / On / In Matrix • Movement • Zero-Article Idioms

Prepositions of Time (At, In, On)

Preposition Governed Time Expressions Textbook Example
AT Clock times, specific moments, noon, night, midnight, weekend "at 5:00 p.m.", "at midnight", "at night"
IN Parts of day (morning, afternoon, evening), months, seasons, years, centuries "in the morning", "in August", "in the summer", "in 2010"
ON Specific calendar dates, days of the week, holidays with "Day" "on Monday", "on August 11", "on my birthday"
FOR / DURING for + length of duration; during + named event / period "for two weeks", "for eight hours", "during the flight"
FROM ... TO / UNTIL Beginning time marker to ending time marker "from 9:00 until noon", "from February 28 to March 15"
Sentence Position Rule (Place before Time):

In standard English sentences, place phrases precede time phrases: "Jemila went to Brazil in January." Time expressions can also sit at the sentence beginning: "In January, Jemila went to Brazil."

Prepositions of Place & Movement

IN (Enclosed Space)

• inside container, car, taxi

• city, state, country, continent

"in Mexico City", "in the closet"

ON (Surface / Street)

• surface, table, shelf, wall

• street, avenue (no number)

• train, bus, plane, ship, bike

"on Fifth Avenue", "on a train"

AT (Point / Address)

• specific point, stop sign

• exact address with number

• events & general locations

"at 21 Millbrook Road", "at the door"

Prepositions of Direction & Movement:
• toward: "walked toward the post office"
• through: "passed through national parks"
• across: "walked across the street"
• between: "walked between moving cars"
• under: "sat under an umbrella"
• from ... to: "drove from Portland to Seattle"
Common Zero-Article Prepositional Idioms

Do NOT add a/an/the in these established expressions:
in class, in bed, at home, at school, at work, by bus, by train, by car, by plane.

Lesson 3 • Charts 4.6 - 4.7

Definite, Indefinite Articles & Generalizations

A / An • The • Zero Article (Ø) • General Statements

Definite vs. Indefinite Article Rules

1. Indefinite Article (A / An):

Used with singular count nouns that are non-specific (any member of a class):

• "She is at a party." (any party, listener doesn't know which)

• Phonetic Rule: a college, a university (/j/ sound) vs. an adventure, an umbrella, an hour (/aʊ/ sound).

2. Non-Specific Plurals & Non-Count (Some or Ø):

Do NOT use a / an with plural count or mass non-count nouns:

• "There are some motorcycles in the parking lot." | "I need some gas."

3. Definite Article (The):

Used with singular count, plural count, or non-count nouns when the item is specific or known:

• Second Mention: "He took a raft with him. He used the raft to cross rivers."

• Shared Knowledge: "When do you have to be at the airport?"

• Unique in Existence: "Look at the sun." | "The weather was perfect."

Generalizations vs. Specific References

A generalization makes a broad statement about all members of a category, rather than a single specific member:

Noun Type Generalization Form Specific Reference Form
Singular Count A / An + Singular:
"A bike is an eco-friendly vehicle."		 The + Singular:
"The bike in the hallway is mine."
Plural Count Ø (No article) + Plural:
"Bikes don’t use any gas."		 The + Plural:
"The bikes are in the garage."
Non-Count Ø (No article) + Non-Count:
"Chocolate is delicious."		 The + Non-Count:
"The chocolate in this cake is rich."
Common Generalization Trap:

✗ The cars are convenient in a big city. (When speaking about cars in general)

✓ Cars are convenient in a big city. OR ✓ A car is convenient in a big city.

Articles with Geographic & Urban Place Names

The vs. Zero Article (Ø) • Geographic Entities • Urban Landmarks

Natural & Political Geography

Category Rule Textbook Examples
Cities, States, Countries, Continents NO ARTICLE (Ø) Portsmouth, Texas, Canada, Africa, Brazil
Places with "of", Republic, or Kingdom USE "THE" the United Kingdom, the Dominican Republic
Plural Geographic Names USE "THE" the United States, the Philippines, the Rocky Mountains, the Alps, the Bahamas
Single Mountain or Single Island NO ARTICLE (Ø) Mount Everest, Mount Rushmore, Long Island, Maui, Bali
Oceans, Seas, Rivers USE "THE" the Atlantic Ocean, the Caspian Sea, the Mississippi River, the Amazon, the Nile
Deserts USE "THE" the Mojave Desert, the Sahara, the Atacama

Urban Landmarks, Streets & Facilities

Category Rule Textbook Examples
Hotels, Theaters, Museums USE "THE" the Plaza Hotel, the Burj Al Arab Hotel, the Sherman Theater, the Museum of Modern Art, the Louvre
Names with Tower, Bridge, Building USE "THE" the Eiffel Tower, the Brooklyn Bridge, the Empire State Building
Streets, Roads, Avenues NO ARTICLE (Ø) Fifth Avenue, Elm Street, North Street, Steamboat Road, Orchard Road
Parks & Public Squares NO ARTICLE (Ø) Central Park, Friendship Park, Union Square, Westridge Park
Airports, Train & Bus Stations NO ARTICLE (Ø) Logan Airport, Heathrow Airport, Haneda Airport, Grand Central Station
Places with "of" or "for":

Institutions with of or for regularly require the: the Statue of Liberty, the Center for Performing Arts, the University of Mumbai.

Avoiding Misplaced Prepositional Phrases

Modifier Placement • Sentence Clarity • Proofreading

When a prepositional phrase is placed in the wrong position within a sentence, the resulting statement becomes awkward, confusing, or unintentionally humorous. In academic English, prepositional modifiers must be placed immediately adjacent to the exact word they describe.

Absurd Physical Placement

The prepositional phrase erroneously modifies the nearest noun:

✗ We waited for the boat inside the café.

(Illogical meaning: The boat is inside the café!)

→ "Inside the café, we waited for the boat."

Misplaced Agency

The modifier separates the action verb from its object or true location:

✗ The man bought a passport in a leather jacket.

(Did the passport wear a leather jacket?)

→ "The man in a leather jacket bought a passport."

Separated Action & Instrument

Place the instrument phrase right after the verb or subject it modifies:

✗ I saw the Eiffel Tower with my camera.

(Does the Eiffel Tower own a camera?)

→ "With my camera, I took a photo of the Eiffel Tower."
Travel & Descriptive Writing Checklist:
Used subject pronouns for subjects and object pronouns after verbs and prepositions.
Selected at (point/time), in (container/city/month), and on (surface/street/date).
Used the for oceans, rivers, deserts, and plural countries; omitted articles for single mountains and cities.
Placed prepositional phrases next to the words they modify to prevent misplaced phrase confusion.
