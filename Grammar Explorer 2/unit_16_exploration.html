Noun Clauses & Reported Speech

1. Noun Clauses with "That"

A noun clause takes the place of a noun in a sentence, serving as the object of a verb. Like any clause, it must contain its own subject and verb.

Verbs of Mental Activity

Common verbs: believe, decide, discover, dream, find out, forget, hope, know, learn, notice, realize, remember, suggest, think, understand.

  • Noun as Object vs. Noun Clause: • Noun Object: "Jennifer realizes the problem."
    • Noun Clause: "Jennifer realizes that her grades are falling."
  • Omission of "That": In speaking and informal writing, that is frequently omitted:
    "I dreamed [that] I was flying."
  • Pronunciation Note: In spoken English, that in a noun clause is unstressed and reduces to a schwa (/ðət/).

Adjectives of Feeling & Expressions

Following Be + Adjective: afraid, certain, concerned, disappointed, glad, happy, sorry, sure, surprised, worried.
• "The students were afraid that the test was today."
Following Introductory Expressions: It is true... or It is a fact...
• "It is a fact that Egypt is in Africa."

2. Noun Clauses with Wh- Words and If/Whether

Wh- Words & Statement Order

Words: when, how, which, where, who, whom, whose, why, what.

THE STATEMENT WORD ORDER RULE! Inside a noun clause, always use Statement Word Order (Subject + Verb). Do NOT use question word order or auxiliary verbs (do/does/did)!
Direct Question: Where is the museum?
Noun Clause: Do you know where the museum is?
Direct Question: When did he leave?
Noun Clause: I don't know when he left.

If / Whether (Yes/No Questions)

Converts direct Yes/No questions into noun clauses.

Direct Question: Does she have class?
Noun Clause: I'm not sure if she has class.
The "Or Not" Placement Rules: • or not can go at the end of the clause:
• "I don't know if he is right or not."
• or not can go directly after whether:
• "I'm not sure whether or not she knows."
Constraint: Never put "or not" directly after "if"! (*if or not)

3. Quoted and Reported Speech

Quoted Speech (Punctuation Rules)

Presents the speaker's exact words enclosed in quotation marks.

1. Comma: Place a comma after the introductory verb (say/ask).
2. Capital: Begin the quote with a capital letter.
3. End Punctuation Inside: Periods and question marks go INSIDE the quotation marks!
Lina asked, "Where is the bus stop?"
Quote First: Use a comma instead of a period for statement quotes ending before the reporting verb: "Your paper is late," the professor said.

Reported Speech & Tense Shifting

Reports what someone said without exact words. Uses a that-clause.

Past Reporting Verb (Backshifting): Simple Present → Simple Past
• Quoted: Julie: "Running is great."
• Reported: Julie said that running was great.
Pronoun & Possessive Shifts: Shift perspective logically:
• Quoted: Joe: "I don't see your map."
• Reported: Joe said he didn't see my map.
Omit Fillers: Omit spoken fillers (um, well, hmm) and direct response words (yes, no) in reported speech!

Reporting Verbs: Said vs. Told

Said (No Direct Object) Use said + noun clause.
Jen said dinner was ready.
Told MUST Have an Object! Use told + object + noun clause. You CANNOT use told without an object!
Jen told us dinner was ready. Jen told dinner was ready.

Writing Focus: Using Quoted Speech for Impact

While reported speech is ideal for summarizing facts, incorporating exact quoted speech in a narrative adds immediacy, emotion, and connects the reader to the moment.

Impact Example: One survivor said, "My family is safe; nothing else really matters."
