Koalas Fight Back!

A surprising defense by an Australian icon

CD2-13
This baby koala is already covered in thick fur. • Queensland, Australia p. 158

In March 2006, thieves broke into a zoo in Queensland, Australia. They planned to steal a koala. Everybody loves koalas, right? With their big¹ round² eyes, flat noses, thick fur coat, and hairy ears, these cute creatures have become a popular symbol of Australia. However, appearances can be deceptive.³ The koala at the zoo was very fierce. Soon the thieves had deep scratches from the koala’s sharp claws,⁴ so they decided to steal something easier. They stole a crocodile instead!

Koalas are interesting animals. A koala mother carries her baby, called a joey, in a warm pouch, or pocket, on her stomach. Koalas eat only the oily leaves of eucalyptus trees. These leaves are harmful to many other animals, but koalas eat about two and a half pounds (just over one kilo) every night. The water content of these leaves is high, so koalas do not drink very much. In fact, in one native⁵ Australian language the word koala means “no drink.”

¹ big: large in size

² round: shaped like a circle

³ deceptive: causing someone to believe something that is not true

⁴ claw: a sharp curved nail on the toe of an animal or bird

⁵ native: belonging to a particular place by birth or natural origin

6.1 Adjectives

Position: Before nouns (a beautiful sunset) or after linking verbs (looks tired, smells delicious).
No Plural Form: Adjectives do NOT take -s (✓ ancient towns, ✗ ancients towns).
Articles: Use a before consonant sounds (a unique experience); an before vowel sounds (an old apartment).

Moko—The Art of Maori Tattoos

Cultural identity, memory, and sacred courage in New Zealand

CD2-15
Dr. Ngahuia Te Awekotuku with her book *Mau Moko: The World of Maori Tattoo* p. 165

Dr. Ngahuia Te Awekotuku of New Zealand is a leading writer on Maori issues in New Zealand. On her chin and lips she proudly wears a Maori tattoo, or moko. The purpose of her moko is to remember the Maori queen who died in 2006.

In the past, young Maori received moko when they became adults. It was an extremely painful process. A sharp tool was used to cut the skin. People sat quietly and patiently to show their courage.

These days, a young Maori might get a moko to mark an achievement¹ such as getting on a sports team or passing an exam. Most young people choose to get a moko using less painful methods than in earlier times.

Some people who are not Maori have also started to get moko. Some Maori don’t mind this, but others are not happy about it. For the Maori, moko remains a very serious tradition.

¹ achievement: something that someone does successfully, usually as a result of much effort and hard work

The Yellow-Tailed Woolly Monkey

Yellow-tailed woolly monkey, Alto Mayo, Amazonas, Peru

Yellow-Tailed Woolly Monkey (*Lagothrix flavicauda*) • Peru p. 174

The yellow-tailed woolly monkey is a very rare animal. It has thick brown fur and white hair around its mouth. The monkey is named for the bright yellow fur underneath its long, curled tail. These monkeys live in a small area in the high mountains of Peru. With their long arms and legs and powerful tail, they move quickly through the forests.

Unfortunately, these monkeys have lost a lot of their natural habitat. Farms and cattle ranches¹ are some of the reasons for this. People are now working hard to protect these amazing animals.

¹ ranch: a large farm used for keeping animals

WRITING FOCUS: Using Adjectives and Adverbs (Page 175)

Good writers use adjectives and adverbs to make their writing more interesting, but choose words carefully and do not use too many adjectives or adverbs.

Compare: "With their big long arms and strong legs and powerful curled tail, they move quickly and gracefully through the beautiful thick Peruvian forests." (Too cluttered!)
