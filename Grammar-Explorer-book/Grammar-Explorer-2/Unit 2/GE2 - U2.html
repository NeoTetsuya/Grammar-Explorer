The Lion Guardians Project, Kenya

Conserving lions with Maasai warriors

CD1-08
A lion in Maasai Mara Game Reserve p. 26

Cattle¹ are basic to the lives of the Maasai people of Kenya. In fact, the Maasai have a saying, “Without the land and the cattle, there are no Maasai people.” In 2006, Maasai cattle herders² in Kenya had a problem. Lions regularly killed their cows. The herders sent warriors³ to hunt and kill the lions. Many lions died. The Maasai were a big threat to the survival of lions in Kenya.

A local organization, Living with Lions, wanted to save lions. They helped train Maasai warriors to protect lions, not kill them. The warriors learned to track the lions with modern technology. They warned herders before the lions got near their cows. The warriors didn’t kill any more lions. Instead, they became Lion Guardians.⁴

The Guardians persuaded the herders not to hunt the lions. They gave each lion a Maasai name, and taught the herders and villagers about them. Local people began to see the lions as individuals. Now, there are more than 30 Lion Guardians. Because of the Guardians’ work, the number of lions that die in their part of Kenya is falling fast.

¹ cattle: cows and bulls

² herder: a person who cares for a large group of one kind of animal

³ warrior: a fighter or soldier

⁴ guardian: a person who protects other people or property

2.1 Simple Past: Statements

Page 28
Affirmative Statements Subject + Verb (Past Form)
I / He / She helped the animals.
You / We / They went to Africa.
Negative Statements Subject + Did Not / Didn't + Base Form
I / He / She didn’t / did not help them.
You / We / They didn’t / did not go to China.
Use the Simple Past for:

• Completed action or event: She called me yesterday.

• Regular or repeated action in the past: I worked every day last week.

• Past states or feelings: He felt sick this morning.

Regular verbs: Add -ed or -d to base form (play ➔ played, dance ➔ danced).

Irregular verbs: Many common verbs have irregular past forms (eat ➔ ate, make ➔ made, go ➔ went, see ➔ saw).

See page A2 for spelling rules and page A4 for a complete list of irregular verbs.

Be Careful! Forms of 'be' (was / were)

The simple past forms of be are was and were (It was warm / It wasn’t cold; They were at work / They weren’t at home).

Do NOT use did not or didn’t with was/were in the negative!

✓ I wasn’t late for class yesterday.

✗ I didn’t was late for class yesterday.

Community Radio in Guatemala

Preserving indigenous Maya languages

CD1-10

Interviewer: So, Angelica, you work as a volunteer¹ at a community² radio station. Why is that an important job in Guatemala today?

¹ volunteer: a person who does work without being paid for it

2.3 Past Progressive: Statements

Page 35
Affirmative Statements Subject + Was/Were + Verb-ing
I / He / She / It was working.
You / We / They were working.
Negative Statements Subject + Was/Were Not + Verb-ing
I / He / She / It was not / wasn't sleeping.
You / We / They were not / weren't sleeping.
Past Progressive Usage:

• Action in progress at a certain time in the past: They were studying at 9:00 last night.

Contrast with Simple Past:

• Yesterday I rode my bike to work. (completed action)

• She swam every day last week. (repeated past action)

• He didn’t like the movie. (past state/feeling)

Non-Action Verbs:

Stative verbs (see, like, understand, own) are not usually used with the progressive.

✓ I saw an accident last night.

✗ I was seeing an accident last night.

Real English: Setting the Scene

The past progressive is often used to describe a scene in the past: The café was full. A band was playing, and people were talking loudly. Everyone was enjoying the evening.

See page A1 for spelling rules for the -ing form of verbs.

Surviving an Avalanche

Jimmy Chin in the Teton Mountains

CD1-11
Teton Mountains • Class 4 Avalanche p. 39

Jimmy Chin is one of the few people to survive a Class 4 avalanche—an avalanche big enough to destroy buildings. In 2011, while he was skiing in the Teton Mountains, an avalanche started behind him. Chin was going down the mountain when, in his own words, “the whole mountainside came down.”

The avalanche buried Chin under tons of snow and carried him downhill. Halfway down, the rolling snow threw him to the surface. However, Chin was not safe. When he looked up, trees were snapping all around him. The snow was still moving fast. Soon the avalanche buried him again.

“I knew I was going to the bottom of the valley,” he remembers. Amazingly,¹ when the avalanche reached the bottom of the mountain, Chin was pushed to the surface! He was alive and on top of the snow when his very surprised friends reached the bottom of the valley.

After this experience, Chin took a month off from work. “It’s important to do what’s meaningful to you,” he says. “You don’t want to take your time for granted.”²

¹ amazingly: (very) surprisingly

² take something for granted: not be grateful enough for something

2.5 Past Time Clauses with When & While

Page 41
Clause Patterns Main Clause & Time Clause
With When:

• The storm began [Main] when I was driving home. [Time Clause]

• When I was driving home, [Time Clause] the storm began. [Main]

• I was sleeping [Main] when you texted me. [Time Clause]

With While:

• The storm began [Main] while we were walking home. [Time Clause]

• While we were walking home, [Time Clause] the storm began. [Main]

• You texted me [Main] while I was sleeping. [Time Clause]

1. Main Clause: Can stand alone as a complete sentence (Elena called last night while I was cooking dinner.).

2. Past Time Clause: Tells when an action happened. Can come before or after main clause (When it started to snow, I was walking home. / It started to snow while I was walking home.).

3. While / When + Past Progressive: Shows an action was in progress when another action happened:

• I broke my leg [2nd Action] when I was skiing. [Action in Progress]

• Iris called [2nd Action] while I was cooking dinner. [Action in Progress]

4. When + Simple Past: Used for an action that happened at a specific point in time:

• I was painting the living room [In Progress] when I fell off the ladder. [Point in Time]

5. Comma Rule: Use a comma when the time clause comes first in the sentence:

• When we were taking the exam, the lights went out. (Comma)

• The lights went out when we were taking the exam. (No comma)

2.6 Events in Sequence: When

Page 42
1. Actions in Sequence (Simple Past in Both):

When two actions happened one after the other, use the simple past in both the time clause and the main clause. Use when to introduce the time clause:

• When her phone rang, she answered it.

➔ Time Clause: 1st Event (phone rang) | Main Clause: 2nd Event (she answered)

2. Clause Position & Meaning:

The past time clause can come first or second in the sentence. The action in the past time clause always happened first:

• When I got home, I took a shower. (1st: got home ➔ 2nd: took a shower)

• I took a shower when I got home. (2nd: took a shower ➔ 1st: got home)

3. Comma Rule:

Remember: Use a comma when the past time clause comes first in the sentence:

• When Joe saw the accident, he called the police.

Who Owns the Past?

British Museum, London: The Parthenon Marbles

CD1-13

Cho: These statues are beautiful. Where are they from?

Noriko: They’re from Athens, Greece. I think they used to be a part of the Parthenon.

Cho: Well, it’s wonderful to see them, but why are they here in London? They should be back in Greece with the rest of the Parthenon.

Noriko: A lot of people agree with you. Lord Elgin, a British diplomat,¹ brought these statues to London in the early nineteenth century.

Cho: You mean he just took them?

Noriko: Well, I don’t know. I’m not sure what really happened. Back then archaeologists² used to do things like that. They would explore a historic site and bring objects from the site back to their home countries. Then, they would study them and put them on display in museums. Sometimes, that’s the only reason treasures like these survived.

Cho: But didn’t the Greek people try to stop Lord Elgin?

Noriko: Sure, there were a lot of complaints. Was it right or wrong to take these statues? It’s a difficult question. I love to visit museums and see things from around the world and learn about them.

Cho: Hmm . . . I understand what you’re saying, but it still seems wrong to me, somehow.

¹ diplomat: a person who acts as the official representative of his or her country in a foreign country

² archaeologist: a person who studies the past by looking at items such as buried houses, tools, pots, etc.

2.7 Used To: Affirmative & Negative Statements

Page 47
Used To Statements Past Habit / State
Affirmative (Subject + used to + Base Form):

• I / She / They used to study history.

Negative (Subject + didn’t use to + Base Form):

• I / She / They didn’t use to study math.

1. Habit or State That No Longer Exists:

• I used to run several times a week. (Now, I don’t.)

• I used to live in a small town. (I live in a city now.)

• I didn’t use to eat meat. (I do now.)

2. Negative Spelling: In negative statements, write didn’t use to (NOT used to):

✓ I didn’t use to love math.

✗ I didn’t used to love math.

Real English

Use used to instead of the simple past to emphasize that the habit or state is not true now:

• When I was young, I used to go to the beach every day.

🗣️ Pronunciation: used to and use to are pronounced /ˈjuːstə/.

Would Statements Would + Base Form
Affirmative (would / 'd):

Remembering a Spring Flood

A personal survival narrative

When I was a young girl, we lived near a river. Every spring when the snow melted, it caused the river to rise a few feet. Then the level of the river would fall again. One spring, however, the river kept rising.

One day that spring, I looked out my window. The river was rising very quickly. My parents came and got me. While the water was rising higher, we climbed onto the roof of our house. From the roof, I looked down at the river. It was covering everything in its path. My family and I waited on our roof for help. I was losing hope when we were finally rescued.

GRAMMAR FOCUS: Simple Past, Past Progressive & Time Clauses (Page 56)

In the narrative story, the writer uses three key grammar structures to tell about a difficult experience:

• Simple Past for completed actions & events:
I looked out my window. My parents came and got me.

• Past Progressive to describe a scene in the past:
The river was rising very quickly.

• Past Time Clauses to describe when actions happened:
When I was a young girl, we lived near a river.

WRITING FOCUS: Sentence Fragments (Page 57)

A sentence fragment is missing an important part of a sentence, such as a subject or verb. Make sure each of your sentences has a subject and a verb.

  • ✗ Waited on our roof for help. (no subject) → ✓ My family and I waited on our roof for help.
  • ✗ Everything in its path. (no subject and no verb) → ✓ It was covering everything in its path.
