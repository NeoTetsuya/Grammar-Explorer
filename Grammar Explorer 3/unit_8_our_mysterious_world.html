1. Present & Future: Possibility and Logic

Possibility (Not Certain)

Use could, may, might, may not, or might not to express that an action or event is possible but you are unsure.

  • The exam could last two hours. I’m not sure.
  • He might not come today.

Logical Conclusions (Very Certain)

A judgment based on existing evidence where you are almost 100% certain it is true.

  • Affirmative (must, have to, have got to) My keys must be in my backpack. I saw them there last night.
  • Negative (must not, couldn't, can't) That couldn't be Bill. Bill is much shorter.

Structural Rules & Constraints

  • Questions: Use could to ask questions about possibility; do not use may or might. (e.g., "Where could my wallet be?")
  • Contractions: Do NOT use contractions for may not or might not.
  • Conclusions vs Facts: Do not use conclusion modals to state known facts. If the sign says "Closed", say "The store is closed", not "The store must be closed".
  • Negative Conclusions: Do NOT use have to or have got to with not to express logical conclusions. Use must not.

2. Progressive Forms: Actions in Progress

Structure: Modal (+ not) + be + verb + -ing

Not Certain

May, might, could (not) + be + -ing

He may be working right now.

Almost Certain

Must (not) + be + -ing

She took her laptop. She must be studying.

Impossible

Can't / Couldn't + be + -ing

His car is here. He can't be driving.

3. The Past: Deductions and History

Structure: Modal (+ not) + have + past participle

Past Possibility

  • Uncertain (may, might, could): Charlie might have done the painting.
  • Impossible (couldn't): Her computer crashed, so she couldn't have finished.

Past Logical Conclusions

  • Affirmative (must have / had to have): She must have loved art. She collected statues.
  • Negative (must not have): He must not have heard the question.

Past Progressive Modals

Modal + have been + verb-ing

Describes a deduction about an action that was ongoing in the past.

Katie’s hair is wet. She must have been swimming.

Pronunciation: Reducing "Have"

In casual speech, "have" is reduced to /əv/ (sounding like "of"), leading to informal spellings.

must have → musta
could have → coulda
might have → mighta

4. Mixed Conditionals & Inverted Conditionals

Mixed Conditionals

Connect a past condition to a present result, or a present state to a past result.

Past Cause → Present Result:
"If I had taken that flight yesterday, I would be in London right now."
Present State → Past Result:
"If I weren't afraid of heights, I would have climbed that mountain."

Inverted Conditionals (Omitting 'If')

In formal writing, omit if and invert the auxiliary verb and subject.

• Standard: If you should require assistance...
• Inverted: Should you require assistance, contact us.
• Standard: If I had known about the delay...
• Inverted: Had I known about the delay, I wouldn't have gone.

Context Matters: The 3 Meanings of "Must"

The meaning of "must" changes entirely based on the context of the sentence.

1. Logical Conclusion "You look pale. You must be sick."
Deduction from evidence
2. Obligation "You must take your medication."
A strict requirement
3. Prohibition (Must not) "You must not smoke here."
Not allowed

Writing Connection: "In fact"

When writing about history or mysterious events, use In fact to emphasize an interesting, surprising, or confirming detail.

Punctuation Rule: Always place a comma directly after In fact when it starts a sentence.

"They might have traveled across the ocean. In fact, evidence suggests they built boats."
