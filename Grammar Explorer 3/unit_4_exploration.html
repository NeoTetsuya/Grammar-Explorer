Negative Yes/No Questions

Expectations, Surprise, and Frustration.

Structure

Attach the contracted -n't to the auxiliary verb at the very beginning of the question.

Isn't he a student?
Didn't she know the answer?
Formal (Uncontracted): Auxiliary + Subject + Not (e.g., Did she not know?)

Why use them?

  • Expecting Agreement "Isn't it a beautiful day?"
  • Expressing Surprise / Annoyance "Haven't you finished that yet?!"

How to Answer

In English, answers agree with the facts of reality, not the negative phrasing of the question.
"Didn't you go to the party?"
→ Yes. (I went to the party.)
→ No. (I didn't go.)

Statement Questions

Informal conversational confirmations.

Structure & Intonation

These are Yes/No questions that maintain normal statement word order. They rely entirely on your voice going up (rising intonation) at the end.

Standard Question (Inverted)
Did you go to Antarctica?
Statement Question (Normal Order)
You went to Antarctica?!
Negative Statement Question
You didn't pack the tent?!
Usage: Exclusively for informal speech. Used to express shock or rapidly confirm information you just heard. Answers follow standard Yes/No patterns (e.g., "Yes, I did.").

Tag Questions: The Polarity Rule

A statement followed by a short two-word question tag, separated by a comma.

[ + ] Affirmative Statement
[ - ] Negative Tag
You are an explorer, aren't you?
She likes hiking, doesn't she?

Expects an affirmative answer: "Yes, I am."

[ - ] Negative Statement
[ + ] Affirmative Tag
You didn't pack the map, did you?
There aren't any bears, are there?

Expects a negative answer: "No, I didn't."

Advanced Tag Rules & Exceptions

  • Verb Agreement: If the statement uses a simple present/past action verb (not 'be'), use do, does, or did in the tag.
    He walked far, didn't he?
  • Negative Adverbs: Words like never, rarely, seldom, hardly make the statement negative, so the tag MUST be affirmative.
    He never listens, does he?
  • The 'I am' Exception: The negative tag for "I am" in standard speech is "aren't I?".
    I am late, aren't I?
  • Indefinite Pronouns: Use the pronoun they for words like everyone, somebody, or nobody.
    Everyone is here, aren't they?
  • Imperatives & Let's: Use will you/would you for commands, and shall we for suggestions.
    Open the door, will you? / Let's go, shall we?

Intonation & Meaning

How you say the tag question completely changes its meaning!

Falling Intonation

The speaker is certain of the answer and simply expects you to agree with them.

Rising Intonation

The speaker is not certain and is genuinely asking to confirm information.

Writing Connection: Dialogue Punctuation

When writing a scene from a story, you must punctuate quotes correctly.

  • Punctuation (commas, periods, question marks) generally goes inside the quotation marks.
  • If a reporting verb (e.g., she said, he asked) interrupts a quote, use commas before and after the reporting block.
"Look," says Pierre slowly, "I don't want to fight."
