1. Clauses of Time and Contrast

The Golden Comma Rule

If the adverb clause comes first in the sentence, use a comma. If it comes after the main clause, do not use a comma.

Time Clauses

Tell when the action in the main clause happens.

  • As / Just as "As" means when or while. "Just as" emphasizes the exact same moment.
    "Just as I was leaving, the phone rang."
  • As long as Means "from beginning to end."
    "I’ll remember it as long as I live."
  • Whenever Means "any time" or "every time."
    "Whenever she speaks, I listen."

Contrast Clauses

Introduce unexpected or surprising information. Use although, even though, or though.

  • Usage Distinctions
    • Even though: Expresses a stronger contrast.
    • Although: Preferred in formal writing.
    • Though: Much more common in informal/spoken English.
  • The "But" Trap

    Never use the conjunction but in a sentence that already uses a contrast clause.

    Although it was raining, but I walked. Although it was raining, I walked.

2. Clauses of Reason and Purpose

Reason Clauses

Explain why something happens. Uses because, since, or now that.

  • Because / Since Introduce direct reasons.
    "Since we are late, let's take a taxi." *Note: Since can also refer to time (e.g., Since 2010).
  • Now that Shows a reason that is true in the present (meaning "because... now").
    "Now that I know English, I can get a job."

Purpose Clauses

Express the goal of an action. Uses so that (meaning "in order to").

  • Using Modals

    Modal verbs like can, could, and would are heavily used inside purpose clauses.

    "We left early so that we can avoid traffic."
  • Real English Tip In casual spoken English, the word that is often omitted.
    "I texted Bill so he could get the info."

3. Reduced Adverb Clauses

Shortening adverb clauses of time and reason into relative phrases.

The Absolute Requirement

You can ONLY reduce an adverb clause if the subject of the adverb clause and the subject of the main clause are the exact same person or thing. Otherwise, you create a "dangling modifier".

Same Subject (Can Reduce) After I studied, I went to bed.
→ After studying, I went to bed.
Different Subjects (Cannot Reduce) After the dog barked, I opened the door.
After barking, I opened the door. (Implies I barked!)

Reducing Time Clauses

Only clauses starting with after, before, since, and while can be reduced.

  • Standard Verbs Omit the subject and change the verb to -ing.
    "Before I leave home..." → "Before leaving home..."
  • Progressive Verbs Omit the subject AND the 'be' verb, keeping only the -ing form.
    "While I was riding..." → "While riding..."

Reducing Reason Clauses

Omit the conjunction (because/since) AND the subject.

  • Standard Reduction
    "Because she is an only child..." → "Being an only child..."
  • Negative Clauses Place not directly before the -ing verb.
    "Since I don't know Chinese..." → "Not knowing Chinese..."
  • Perfect Aspect (Past/Present Perfect) Change have/had to having.
    "Since she had spent her money..." → "Having spent her money..."

Writing Connection: Making Comparisons with "Like"

Use the prepositional phrase like + a noun phrase to show that the subject of your main clause is similar to the noun following like.

Punctuation Rule:

Always place a comma after the phrase with like when it starts a sentence.

"Like any city, Seoul can be a stressful place to live."
